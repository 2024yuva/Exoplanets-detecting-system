The basics

What Is an Exoplanet?

A planet, but not ours

An exoplanet is simply a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. Just like Earth orbits the Sun, exoplanets orbit their own distant stars.

Thousands discovered

Since the first confirmed discovery in 1992, astronomers have found over 5,500 exoplanets. Many more await confirmation.

Every size and shape

Some are rocky like Earth, others are gas giants far bigger than Jupiter. Some orbit so close to their star they glow red-hot.

The method

How Do We Detect Exoplanets?

Most exoplanets are found using the transit method — watching a star for tiny dips in brightness when a planet passes in front of it.

Watch the planet cross in front of the star...

Light curve

Star brightness over time

When the planet crosses in front of its star, it blocks a tiny amount of light. This creates a "dip" in the light curve — that dip is how we know a planet is there.

Where AI meets astronomy

Our AI Connection

Real astronomical data is incredibly noisy. Machine learning helps us find faint planet signals hidden in the chaos.

Light Curve

Raw brightness measurements from a telescope — full of noise, instrument errors, and cosmic interference.

Preprocessing

The data is cleaned, normalized, and prepared — removing systematic errors while preserving real signals.

AI Model

A deep-learning neural network analyzes the processed light curve, trained to recognize transit patterns.

Prediction

The model outputs a probability: Is there a planet here? High-confidence signals become new exoplanet candidates.

From Noise to Discovery

Our project uses this exact pipeline. We take noisy TESS/Kepler light curves, denoise them with autoencoders, detect transit dips, and classify candidates — all powered by machine learning. The visualizations you explored above are the output of this AI-driven process.
